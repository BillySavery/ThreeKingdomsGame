    How to play. Officers of the era were known by a courtesy name as well as their given name. Each day shows five courtesy names and the five officers who went by them, in a jumbled order. Drag the officers (or tap two to swap them) so each one sits next to their courtesy name, then check your answers. Right matches lock in, and you get three checks in all. Officers of the era were known by a courtesy name as well as their given name. Each day shows five courtesy names. Name the officer behind each one, with three guesses a name. A first guess scores a point, a second half a point and a third a quarter. Your first miss reveals their faction, and your second reveals their family name, the name that went with the courtesy name, so Cao Mengde for Cao Cao. If only one or two officers share that family name, you get their role and year of death instead, so the hint never gives the answer away. The first three are well known figures, the last two go deeper. Every name is checked against Wikipedia, which cites the historical records, and courtesy names shared by more than one person are left out.

    Reading the names. The game is written in English. Courtesy names are spelled in pinyin, and the Chinese characters read left to right, the way modern Chinese is printed across a page. Older Chinese texts, including the original records and the novel, run in columns from top to bottom and right to left.
